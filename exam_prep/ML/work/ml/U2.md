### Definition
**K-Means** is an **unsupervised, centroid-based (partitioning) clustering** algorithm that divides n data points into **K clusters**, where each point belongs to the cluster with the **nearest centroid (mean)**. It minimises the **Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS)**.

### 1. Centroid-Based Clustering
- Each cluster is represented by a **central vector (centroid)**; points are assigned to the closest centroid.
- K must be given **in advance**; it is an **iterative**, **flat (non-hierarchical)** method.
- Types: **K-Means** (centroid = mean), **K-Medoids/PAM** (centroid = actual data point), **K-Means++** (smart initialisation), Fuzzy C-Means.

### 2. K-Means Algorithm
1. Choose the number of clusters **K**.
2. **Initialise** K centroids randomly (or with K-Means++).
3. **Assign** each point to the **nearest centroid** (Euclidean distance).
4. **Update** each centroid = **mean** of points assigned to it.
5. **Repeat** steps 3–4 until centroids **do not change** (convergence) or max iterations reached.

$ Objective: J = Σₖ Σ_{x ∈ Cₖ} ‖x − μₖ‖²
- **Cₖ** = k-th cluster, **μₖ** = its centroid, **‖x − μₖ‖** = Euclidean distance.

![Fig. K-Means with K = 3: points grouped around centroids C1, C2, C3](kmeans){8}
- **Coloured points:** members of each cluster. **X marks:** centroids (means). Boundaries lie midway between centroids.

### 3. Worked Example (1-D, K = 2)
Data: {2, 4, 10, 12, 3, 20, 30, 11, 25}; initial centroids **m₁ = 2, m₂ = 4**.
| Iteration | Cluster 1 | Cluster 2 | New m₁ | New m₂ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | {2, 3} | {4, 10, 12, 20, 30, 11, 25} | 2.5 | 16 |
| 2 | {2, 3, 4} | {10, 12, 20, 30, 11, 25} | 3 | 18 |
| 3 | {2, 3, 4, 10} | {12, 20, 30, 11, 25} | 4.75 | 19.6 |
| 4 | {2, 3, 4, 10, 12, 11} | {20, 30, 25} | 7 | 25 |
| 5 | {2, 3, 4, 10, 12, 11} | {20, 30, 25} | 7 | 25 (no change → stop) |
**Final clusters:** {2, 3, 4, 10, 11, 12} and {20, 25, 30}. (In iteration 1, point 3 is equidistant from 2 and 4; the tie is given to cluster 1.)

### 4. Advantages
- **Simple**, easy to implement and interpret.
- **Fast & scalable** – O(n·K·t); works on large datasets.
- Guaranteed to **converge**; tight, spherical clusters.
### 5. Disadvantages
- **K must be specified** in advance (use elbow/silhouette).
- **Sensitive to initial centroids** (different runs → different results) and to **outliers**.
- Assumes **spherical, equal-size** clusters; fails on non-convex shapes.
- Works only with **numerical** data (mean must exist).

### 6. Applications
- Customer segmentation, image compression (colour quantisation), document clustering.

### Conclusion
K-Means repeatedly **assigns** points to the nearest mean and **updates** the means until stable; it is fast and popular but needs K and good initialisation.
> **Memory trick – "CAUR":** **C**hoose K → **A**ssign → **U**pdate → **R**epeat. Common mistake: forgetting the stopping condition.

### Definition
**DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise)** groups points that lie in **dense regions** and marks points in **low-density regions as noise (outliers)**. It needs two parameters: **ε (eps)** – neighbourhood radius and **MinPts** – minimum points to form a dense region.

### 1. Key Terms
- **ε-neighbourhood:** all points within distance ε of a point.
- **Core point:** has **≥ MinPts** points (including itself) in its ε-neighbourhood.
- **Border point:** fewer than MinPts neighbours but lies in the ε-neighbourhood of a core point.
- **Noise point:** neither core nor border.
- **Directly density-reachable:** q is in ε-neighbourhood of core point p.
- **Density-reachable:** reachable through a chain of core points. **Density-connected:** both reachable from a common core point.

![Fig. DBSCAN: core, border and noise points with ε-radius](dbscan){9}
- **Blue (dashed circle):** core point and its ε-radius containing ≥ MinPts points.
- **Orange square:** border point (inside a core's ε but not dense itself).
- **Red ×:** noise / outliers – not assigned to any cluster.

### 2. Algorithm / Working
1. Set **ε** and **MinPts**; mark all points unvisited.
2. Pick an **unvisited point p**; mark it visited and find its ε-neighbours.
3. If neighbours **< MinPts** → mark p as **noise** (may later become border).
4. If neighbours **≥ MinPts** → p is a **core point**; start a **new cluster** and add all neighbours.
5. **Expand:** for each neighbour that is also a core point, add its neighbours to the cluster (density-reachable points).
6. Repeat until all points are visited. Clusters = **density-connected** sets.

### 3. Example
Customer GPS locations: dense shopping areas form clusters of any shape (along roads); isolated locations are flagged as **noise**. With ε = 1 km, MinPts = 5.

### 4. Advantages
- **No need to specify the number of clusters.**
- Finds clusters of **arbitrary shape** (non-spherical).
- **Robust to outliers** – identifies noise explicitly.
- Only two parameters; order of data has little effect.
### 5. Disadvantages
- Choosing **ε and MinPts** is difficult (k-distance graph helps).
- Fails when clusters have **varying densities**.
- Poor in **high dimensions** (distance becomes less meaningful); O(n²) without index.
- Border points may be assigned to different clusters depending on order.

### Conclusion
DBSCAN clusters by **density**, finding arbitrary-shaped clusters and outliers automatically, but is sensitive to ε and MinPts.
> **Memory trick – "C-B-N":** **C**ore (dense), **B**order (edge), **N**oise (alone). "Two knobs: **ε** = how far, **MinPts** = how many."

### Definition
A **Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)** is a **probabilistic, distribution-based clustering** model which assumes data is generated from a **mixture of K Gaussian (normal) distributions**, each with its own **mean μₖ, covariance Σₖ and mixing weight πₖ**. It performs **soft clustering** – each point gets a **probability** of belonging to each cluster.

### 1. Model
$ p(x) = Σₖ πₖ · N(x | μₖ, Σₖ) ,   Σₖ πₖ = 1
- **πₖ** = mixing coefficient (prior weight of cluster k), **μₖ** = mean (centre), **Σₖ** = covariance (shape/orientation), **N** = Gaussian density.

![Fig. GMM: two Gaussian components with elliptical contours](gmm){9}
- **Ellipses:** 1σ and 2σ contours of each Gaussian – clusters can be **elliptical** and of different sizes.
- **+ marks:** means μₖ. Points in the overlap get split membership probabilities.

### 2. EM Algorithm for GMM
1. **Initialise** μₖ, Σₖ, πₖ (randomly or from K-Means).
2. **E-step (Expectation):** compute **responsibility** γ(zₙₖ) = probability that point xₙ belongs to cluster k:
$ γₙₖ = πₖ N(xₙ | μₖ, Σₖ) / Σⱼ πⱼ N(xₙ | μⱼ, Σⱼ)
3. **M-step (Maximisation):** update parameters using responsibilities:
   - μₖ = Σₙ γₙₖ xₙ / Nₖ ; Σₖ = Σₙ γₙₖ (xₙ − μₖ)(xₙ − μₖ)ᵀ / Nₖ ; πₖ = Nₖ / N, where Nₖ = Σₙ γₙₖ.
4. Compute **log-likelihood**; repeat E and M steps until it **converges**.
5. Assign each point to the cluster with the **highest probability** (if hard labels needed).

### 3. Example
Heights of a mixed group of adults: two overlapping bell curves – women (μ ≈ 160 cm) and men (μ ≈ 175 cm). GMM learns both Gaussians; a person of 168 cm may get **0.45 women / 0.55 men**.

### 4. How GMM differs from other clustering algorithms
| Basis | GMM | K-Means / DBSCAN / Hierarchical |
|---|---|---|
| Assignment | **Soft** (probabilities) | **Hard** (one cluster) |
| Cluster shape | **Elliptical**, different sizes (covariance) | K-Means: spherical; DBSCAN: arbitrary |
| Basis | **Statistical distribution** (likelihood) | Distance (K-Means, HC) or density (DBSCAN) |
| Algorithm | **EM** (E-step, M-step) | Assign–update / region growing / merge-split |
| Output | μ, Σ, π + membership probabilities | Labels (and centroids) |
| Generative | Yes – can generate new samples | No |
| K required | Yes (choose by BIC/AIC) | K-Means yes; DBSCAN no |

### 5. Advantages / Disadvantages
- **+** Flexible cluster shapes; soft membership; probabilistic interpretation; density estimation.
- **−** Needs K; sensitive to initialisation; can converge to local optimum; slower than K-Means; assumes Gaussian data.

### Conclusion
GMM models data as a **weighted sum of Gaussians** learnt by **EM**, giving **soft, elliptical** clusters – a probabilistic generalisation of K-Means.
> **Memory trick:** "**E** = Estimate who belongs, **M** = Move the Gaussians." GMM = "**soft K-Means with shapes**".

### Definition
**Clustering** is an **unsupervised learning** technique that groups **unlabelled** data objects so that objects in the **same cluster are similar** (high intra-cluster similarity) and objects in **different clusters are dissimilar** (low inter-cluster similarity).

### 1. Characteristics of good clustering
- High **intra-cluster** similarity, low **inter-cluster** similarity.
- Scalable, handles different attribute types, noise and arbitrary shapes; interpretable.

![Fig. Types of clustering methods](cluster_types){14}

### 2. Types of Clustering
1. **Partitioning / Centroid-based clustering**
   - Divides data into **K non-overlapping** groups; each represented by a centroid.
   - Examples: **K-Means, K-Medoids (PAM), CLARA**.
   - Fast; needs K; spherical clusters.
2. **Hierarchical (connectivity-based) clustering**
   - Builds a **tree of clusters (dendrogram)**.
   - **Agglomerative (AGNES)** – bottom-up merging; **Divisive (DIANA)** – top-down splitting.
   - No need for K in advance; costly O(n²).
3. **Density-based clustering**
   - Clusters = **dense regions** separated by sparse regions; finds arbitrary shapes and noise.
   - Examples: **DBSCAN, OPTICS, Mean-shift**.
4. **Distribution (model)-based clustering**
   - Assumes data comes from **probability distributions**; soft membership.
   - Example: **Gaussian Mixture Model (EM)**.
5. **Grid-based clustering**
   - Quantises space into a **grid of cells** and clusters the cells; very fast.
   - Examples: **STING, CLIQUE**.
6. **Fuzzy (soft) clustering**
   - A point can belong to **several clusters with membership degrees** (0–1).
   - Example: **Fuzzy C-Means**.
- *Also:* **Hard vs soft** clustering; **flat vs hierarchical**.

### 3. Example
Online store groups customers by spending and visits: K-Means → 3 segments (budget, regular, premium); DBSCAN flags bulk-buying outliers.

| Type | Idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Partitioning | Nearest centroid | K-Means |
| Hierarchical | Merge / split tree | AGNES, DIANA |
| Density | Dense regions | DBSCAN |
| Distribution | Mixture of Gaussians | GMM |
| Grid | Cells of space | STING |
| Fuzzy | Membership degrees | FCM |

### Conclusion
Clustering finds natural groups in unlabelled data; the type is chosen according to cluster **shape, size, noise and data volume**.
> **Memory trick – "PHD-DG-F":** **P**artitioning, **H**ierarchical, **D**ensity, **D**istribution, **G**rid, **F**uzzy.

### Definition
**Clustering** groups unlabelled data into meaningful groups. The **Elbow method** is a graphical technique to choose the **optimal number of clusters K** for K-Means by plotting **WCSS** against K and picking the "**elbow**" point.

### 1. Need of Clustering
1. **Labelled data is costly** – clustering finds structure without labels.
2. **Pattern discovery:** reveals hidden groups (customer types, gene families).
3. **Data summarisation / reduction:** represent many points by few centroids (compression).
4. **Pre-processing** for other algorithms (features, sampling, semi-supervised labelling).
5. **Outlier/anomaly detection:** points far from all clusters.
6. **Decision making:** targeted marketing, resource allocation, recommendation.

### 2. Elbow Method – Steps
1. Run K-Means for K = 1, 2, …, 10.
2. For each K compute **WCSS** (inertia):
$ WCSS = Σₖ Σ_{x ∈ Cₖ} ‖x − μₖ‖²
   - **μₖ** = centroid of cluster Cₖ; ‖·‖ = Euclidean distance.
3. **Plot** WCSS (y-axis) vs K (x-axis).
4. WCSS always decreases as K increases (K = n → WCSS = 0).
5. Choose K at the **"elbow"** – where the decrease **sharply slows down**; adding more clusters gives little gain.

![Fig. Elbow curve: WCSS drops sharply till K = 3, then flattens](elbow){9}
- **x-axis:** number of clusters K; **y-axis:** WCSS.
- **Elbow (K = 3):** best trade-off between compactness and number of clusters.

### 3. Example
WCSS = 1000, 600, 300, 230, 190 for K = 1…5 → drop 400, 300, then only 70 → **K = 3**.

### 4. Limitations
- Elbow may be **unclear/smooth**; then use **silhouette score** or gap statistic.

### Conclusion
Clustering is needed to discover structure in unlabelled data, and the elbow method gives a simple visual way to choose K for K-Means.
> **Memory trick:** "Bend your **arm** – the **elbow** is where the curve bends." Plot **WCSS vs K**, not accuracy.

### Definition
**Hierarchical clustering** builds a **hierarchy (tree) of clusters** shown as a **dendrogram**. **Agglomerative (AHC/AGNES)** works **bottom-up** by merging; **Divisive (DHC/DIANA)** works **top-down** by splitting.

![Fig. Dendrogram: agglomerative merges upward, divisive splits downward](dendrogram){10}

| Basis | Agglomerative (AHC) | Divisive (DHC) |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | **Bottom-up** | **Top-down** |
| Start | Each point is its **own cluster** (n clusters) | **All points in one** cluster |
| Operation | **Merge** the two closest clusters | **Split** a cluster into two |
| End | One cluster (or K clusters) | Each point alone (or K clusters) |
| Decision based on | **Linkage**: single, complete, average, Ward | Largest **diameter** / dissimilarity (DIANA), or K-Means split (bisecting) |
| Complexity | O(n² log n) to O(n³) | O(2ⁿ) exhaustive; heuristics needed |
| Accuracy | Local decisions; early merges cannot be undone | Uses **global view** first → can be more accurate at top levels |
| Popularity | **More common**, easy to implement | Less common, complex |
| Algorithm name | AGNES | DIANA |
| Good for | Finding small clusters | Finding large clusters |

### Example
Points A–E: AHC merges (A,B), (D,E), then C with (D,E), then all. DHC first splits {A..E} into {A,B} and {C,D,E}, then continues splitting.

### Conclusion
Both produce a dendrogram; **AHC merges** small clusters upward, **DHC splits** the whole set downward. Cut the dendrogram at a height to get K clusters.
> **Memory trick:** **A**gglomerative = **A**dd together (bottom-up); **D**ivisive = **D**ivide (top-down).

### Definition
**Divisive Hierarchical Clustering (DHC / DIANA – DIvisive ANAlysis)** is a **top-down** clustering method: it starts with **all points in one cluster** and **recursively splits** clusters until each point is a separate cluster or a stopping criterion (K clusters) is met.

### 1. Algorithm (DIANA)
1. Start with **one cluster** containing all n points; compute the **distance matrix**.
2. Choose the cluster with the **largest diameter** (max distance between any two of its points).
3. In that cluster, find the point with the **highest average dissimilarity** to the others → it starts a new **"splinter group"**.
4. **Move** points that are closer (on average) to the splinter group than to the remaining group into the splinter group.
5. Repeat step 4 until no point moves → the cluster is split into **two**.
6. Repeat steps 2–5 until every cluster has **one point** (or K clusters reached).
7. Draw the **dendrogram** (top-down).
- *Alternative:* **Bisecting K-Means** – split the largest cluster with K-Means (K = 2) repeatedly.

![Fig. Divisive clustering: splitting {A..E} step by step](divisive){11}

### 2. Example
1-D points: A = 1, B = 2, C = 6, D = 9, E = 10.
- **Step 1:** {A,B,C,D,E}. Average distance of A to others = (1+5+8+9)/4 = **5.75** (highest) → A starts splinter group.
- B: avg to splinter {A} = 1, to rest {C,D,E} = (4+7+8)/3 = 6.33 → **B moves**. C: to {A,B} = 4.5, to {D,E} = 3.5 → stays.
- **Split 1:** {A, B} and {C, D, E}.
- **Step 2:** largest diameter is {C,D,E} (10 − 6 = 4) → C is farthest (avg 3.5) → **{C}** and **{D, E}**.
- **Step 3:** split {A,B} → {A},{B}; split {D,E} → {D},{E}. Done.

### 3. Advantages / Disadvantages
- **+** Takes a **global view** first; good at finding **large clusters**; no K needed (cut dendrogram).
- **−** Computationally **expensive**; splits cannot be undone; sensitive to outliers.

### Conclusion
DHC (DIANA) starts from one cluster and keeps **splitting the widest cluster** using the splinter-group idea until all points are separated, producing a top-down dendrogram.
> **Memory trick:** "**DIANA divides** – find the **odd one out** (splinter), pull its friends, split."

### Definition
**Clustering** groups similar unlabelled objects; because it needs **no labels**, it is widely used to discover segments, communities, regions and outliers in real data.

![Fig. Major applications of clustering](cluster_apps){10}

### 1. Market Segmentation
- Group customers by **demographics, purchase history, spending, frequency** (RFM).
- Algorithm: **K-Means / GMM**.
- **Example:** a supermarket finds 3 segments – *budget shoppers, regular families, premium buyers* – and sends targeted offers to each.

### 2. Social Network Analysis
- Detect **communities** – groups of users who interact more with each other than with others.
- Algorithm: **hierarchical / graph (spectral) clustering**.
- **Example:** Facebook/LinkedIn suggest friends or groups by clustering users with common connections and interests; detecting fake-account rings.

### 3. Image Segmentation
- Group **pixels** with similar colour, intensity or texture into regions.
- Algorithm: **K-Means on pixel colours**, mean-shift, DBSCAN.
- **Example:** in an MRI scan, separating **tumour tissue** from healthy tissue; separating road, sky and vehicles in self-driving car images; colour quantisation.

### 4. Anomaly (Outlier) Detection
- Points that **do not belong to any cluster** or lie **far from centroids** are anomalies.
- Algorithm: **DBSCAN (noise points)**, distance from K-Means centroid.
- **Example:** credit-card **fraud** – a transaction far from a customer's usual cluster (amount, location, time) is flagged; network intrusion detection.

### 5. Other Applications (brief)
- Document/news grouping, recommendation systems, biology (gene expression), city planning, insurance.

| Application | What is clustered | Typical algorithm | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market segmentation | Customers | K-Means, GMM | Budget vs premium buyers |
| Social network analysis | Users / nodes | Hierarchical, spectral | Friend communities |
| Image segmentation | Pixels | K-Means, mean-shift | Tumour region in MRI |
| Anomaly detection | Transactions / events | DBSCAN | Card fraud |

### Conclusion
Clustering turns raw unlabelled data into **actionable groups** – segments for marketing, communities in networks, regions in images and outliers for security.
> **Memory trick – "MSIA":** **M**arket, **S**ocial, **I**mage, **A**nomaly. Always give one concrete example per application.
